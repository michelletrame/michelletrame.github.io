The problem

Growing pains and interpersonal conflict

{% include figure.html src="hack-reactor/josh-and-dan" alt="Two technical mentors in Hack Reactor T-shirts lean over a table to talk with a group of four students at their laptops." caption="Technical mentors Josh and Dan advising a thesis group." %}

As we grew from cohorts of 24 students to cohorts of 40, conflict in group projects went from a minor stressor to a major source of frustration and resentment. We didn't track how often students cried in the hallways, but if we had, I'm sure it would have shot up.

For staff, what had been a back-burner issue became an outsized drain on the time technical mentors had for teaching. Learning to manage conflict is an important skill, but the amount of it was distracting students from why they came: learning to code.

{% include quote.html text="I'm spending all my time helping them talk to each other and we haven't even looked at their code." who="Maddie, student mentor" %}

Staff observations

Can we design more perfect teams?

  • Bigger cohorts meant we were less in tune with interpersonal dynamics that were becoming exponentially more complex.
  • Students blamed staff for putting them in "bad groups," instead of learning to work well with their teams.
  • Teams in intense conflict weren't necessarily learning from it. They were just flailing.

Research

Collecting the right data

We realized we needed different data from students to understand the causes of the problem. We needed:

  • More actionable preference data
  • A way to identify people who were struggling to collaborate before they were placed in project groups
  • A more nuanced picture of what struggling students were experiencing
  1. Students list up to 8 people they'd like to work with.→Students give a preference for every other student in the cohort.
  2. Staff take notes on students' ability to collaborate; multiple red flags go to the leadership team.→If students name someone they won't work with, they say whether the reason is interpersonal or technical.
  3. Staff step in to adjudicate and fix things when groups run into trouble.→Staff meet early with students flagged as challenging, to set clear expectations about project work and the support we can offer.

Insights

How were students processing their experience?

  • Students were annoyed when the preferences they gave staff weren't reflected in the group they got.
  • Students were stressed about not knowing how their preferences turned into project groups.
  • Students were making conspiratorial assumptions about outcomes that were coincidental or unintentional.

When we tried to support struggling groups, many members resisted. They explained their situation through what social psychologists call the fundamental attribution error: the problem wasn't their interpersonal skills, it was the situation they'd been put in, a bad group.

Students like Brandon who struggled technically had always been hard to place, because nobody listed them as a preference. His group resented that he couldn't contribute at their level, and that nobody had chosen to work with him. His lack of self-awareness about his technical limits made collaborating even harder.

Without a transparent system for turning preferences into groups, students like Eduardo developed theories about elaborate social engineering behind the groups we created.

Reframe

It's not about the group, it's about the black box

{% include figure.html src="hack-reactor/students-mylani" alt="Three students grin at the camera from their desks during a pair programming session." caption="Students pair programming in the first half of the course." %}

As we talked with students, we realized that the opaque process was shaping how they explained their stress to themselves. If we opened it up and gave them insight into how we worked, we believed they'd take ownership of their group dynamics.

Before, data about who ranked low was hidden because it wasn't actionable: we usually didn't know whether a low score came from interpersonal issues, technical ones or both. Better data let us support struggling students with specialized study plans, or even have them work independently so they could focus fully on technical growth rather than project management and collaboration.

With better data we also wanted to systematize the process, and make it obvious that the result came from trying to honor preferences across the whole cohort, not from optimizing for something else.

Co-design

Bringing students in as co-designers

{% include figure.html src="hack-reactor/me-at-hack-reactor" alt="Michelle leans over a student's shoulder to look at code on his monitor while other students work nearby." caption="Looking at Adam's code." %}

We started by inviting students into how we currently assigned project groups. We were open that we were still iterating, and shared how we'd already changed the way we collected data.

Then we announced a mini hackathon and invited students to help us write code to solve the problem. We briefed them on why we'd changed how we collected preferences, what we wanted from a solution (the most happiness for the whole cohort, without sacrificing any group), and gave them anonymized datasets. Ten perfectly cohesive four-person teams might not be possible, but we wanted to reframe group-making as a collaboration, not an edict. Solving it together also helped students see how complex this seemingly simple task is.

A lightweight first pass

I joined the hackathon as a participant too. I wasn't as savvy with complex code as many of the students, so I used my familiarity with the problem to build a spreadsheet solution. Working alongside them, I saw how they prioritized and solved problems, and they saw how invested I was in solving it with them.

To form highly compatible groups, I wrote a very lightweight algorithm: it scored students using the new preference data, sorted them by score, and matched low scorers with high scorers who had expressed a preference for them. I'd used this approach with some success before, with the old data.

Outcome

Less anxiety about groups, more ownership

The problem we solved wasn't the problem we started with.

We started out focused on the outcome, good groups, but research and co-design showed that the biggest pain point was the process. Opening it up to students had a huge impact. It changed how they understood their responsibility for team dynamics, and ended the conspiracy theories about social engineering behind their backs. The hackathon produced a Python script that we combined with my simple algorithm into a rough but workable, efficient process for forming project groups.

{% include figure.html src="hack-reactor/hr-bbp-5307-casual" alt="Three students talk and laugh around a laptop at a shared table." %}

Earlier, better support for students who struggle

Because this worked so well at directing support to students who struggled in teams, we started collecting interpersonal data in the first half of the course too, when students pair program. Some of the support strategies we'd built for struggling students in the second half grew into feedback and reflection practices that every student took part in during the first half.
