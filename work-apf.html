Context

Who are parks for?

APF's strategic plan listed 13 stakeholders, from the City of Austin to donors, vendors and the media. One group was missing: park users.

APF's mission is to improve parks for the people who use them, but its day-to-day operations rarely involve talking to those people. Our team set out to bridge that gap.

{% include figure.html src="apf/dog-walker" alt="A woman walks her dog in the distance down a wide paved path under a canopy of trees." caption="A park user walking her dog in a popular Austin park." %}

We started with in-context generative research with 20 people in 16 different Austin parks. We focused on observing what people do, to understand their values and motivations. As well as shadowing them on park visits, we led them through exercises such as drawing a mental map of Austin's parks.

{% include figure.html src="apf/apf-timeline-wide-01" diagram=true alt="Project timeline from August to December: contextual research in August, theme finding in September, system mapping in October, insights and problem statements in November, and design criteria in December." caption="Project timeline." %}

Research

Increasing community buy-in

{% include figure.html src="apf/participant-tumbnails" variant="inset" alt="A grid of 20 candid photos of research participants in Austin parks: walking trails, sketching maps at picnic tables, browsing a little free library and pointing out favorite spots." caption="Our participants." %}

Most of the park users we talked with wanted to be more involved with their park, either by giving money or by volunteering. Yet most weren't actually doing it. Why? As a non-profit, APF depends on community support, so we wanted to understand what separated people who actively supported its work from people who cared deeply but hadn't acted.

APF's narrow messaging about volunteers meant the people we talked to thought getting involved was only about physical labor. Leaders inside APF knew other skills (event production, web development, activism) would benefit the organization, but there were no pathways for community members to contribute in those ways.

We spoke with people passionate about organizing events, gatherings and meetups who felt the Parks Department, and by extension APF, was actively hostile to their efforts. These event organizers were also the users most successful at cultivating an inclusive view of parks.

Synthesis

Unearthing patterns of exclusion

We affinitized hours of transcripts to find patterns in values, beliefs, motivations and behaviors, and several themes emerged. Parks can be a "third place," shared and cared for by the whole community, yet exclusion came up again and again. Within an hour or two, people began revealing ways they had experienced or perpetuated exclusion in parks, often with narrow, specific criteria for who belongs and what they should be doing there.

It wasn't all bad news. We also saw clear patterns that countered these views: park users, and types of parks, that fostered an expansive view of who belongs.

Going deeper

A deep dive with super users

Our main focus was the average park user, but we also interviewed Park Adopters, APF's most engaged volunteers. Their perspective mattered because APF works with them directly, and because they have an outsized influence on how park resources are allocated.

Mapping power relationships among Park Adopters showed how current systems were perpetuating inequality, and where those patterns could be disrupted. Being in a well-resourced area correlated with better outcomes for a park, but another variable also shaped a volunteer's leverage: how long they'd been a Park Adopter.

{% include figure.html src="apf/complexity-curve" diagram=true alt="A complexity curve: the y-axis is complexity, the x-axis is time. Three park adopters, Rico, Robert and Daniel, sit along the curve. Robert, in the middle, is connected to the most organizations, including APF, the City of Austin, Watershed, 311 and a conservation group, at the curve's peak. Rico, early on, and Daniel, later, have fewer connections." caption="The complexity curve we developed while mapping systems of power within the parks." %}

Surprisingly, the relationship between time and influence wasn't linear. The volunteers struggling most weren't the newest. Mid-stage Park Adopters hit peak complexity: they had found many avenues for help but hadn't yet narrowed them to the most effective ones, as their more experienced counterparts had. That overwhelm often came before people disengaged, except for adopters with a strong network of people doing similar civic work.

Insights

Identifying the roadblocks

We developed three insights about what was stopping APF from serving park users equitably and inclusively, while nurturing its relationship with Austin's communities.

Design criteria

Nine criteria for an inclusive APF

We turned the three insights into nine design criteria that any future APF program could be measured against.

Vibrant park identities that…

Communicate purpose
Use physical cues in parks to communicate what a green space is for, and the values and boundaries expected there.
Grant acceptance
Take a strong stance on the right of people experiencing homelessness to use public green space.
Align with specific uses
Create experiences that draw people from across the city into parks outside their own neighborhoods.

Volunteer opportunities that…

Prioritize autonomy
Open-ended, non-prescriptive opportunities encourage people with unique interests and talents to get involved.
Consider physical abilities
Visible programs for people of all abilities counter the idea that volunteering is only physical work.
Leverage self-interest
Projects can meet the volunteer's needs (building a portfolio, meeting neighbors) and APF's (free, specialized labor).

Equal access to resources by…

Collecting use-based donations
Let people give based on their interests and how they use parks, rather than to a specific park.
Distributing social capital
Create Park Adopter teams that represent a demographically diverse portfolio of parks, not a single park.
Balancing voices
Neighborhood associations and political squeaky wheels are overrepresented in privileged areas. Gather feedback directly from residents to rebalance.

Recommendation

Park Adopters as change agents

Iterating on the Park Adopter program would have an outsized impact because APF controls it completely, unlike programs it runs jointly with the Parks and Recreation Department.

{% include figure.html src="apf/park-adopter-structure" diagram=true alt="Two diagrams side by side. On the left, one neighborhood park has one Park Adopter, who connects to non-profit partners, neighbors, event organizers, local government and donors. On the right, the same structure with two or more Park Adopters for one park." caption="The existing Park Adopter model (left) and the nearly identical model APF was starting to pilot in some parks (right)." %}

Park Adopters working within the existing model faced several challenges:

  • Not enough bandwidth to become experts at working with every relevant agency
  • Vast differences in social and political capital between adopters
  • Adopters who didn't represent what their neighborhood wanted and needed
  • Unequal access to mentorship

Giving one park two or more adopters was meant to make adoption more representative. But it didn't disrupt geographic inequities, and in some cases it set up power struggles between devoted adopters with different visions for their park, deepening an "us versus them" dynamic in rapidly gentrifying neighborhoods.

A park portfolio model

We recommended a Park Adopter Portfolio: small teams of five to seven people, each looking after a group of parks deliberately chosen across socio-economic and cultural lines. Small enough to stay nimble, big enough for people to specialize.

Rico, the all-star neighborhood morale builder, could share his expertise with Daniel and Robert, who sometimes struggled to engage their neighbors, or simply take on neighbor communications for the whole group. Politically connected Daniel could introduce Rico and Robert to the right people, or lobby City Hall on behalf of any park in the portfolio.

APF had hoped for this kind of cross-pollination at its annual Park Adopter training, but that was a single, not always well-attended weekend. An ongoing partnership with mutual investment and expectations of reciprocal support would change how people advocate for "their" park.

{% include figure.html src="apf/park-portfolio-model" diagram=true alt="Diagram of the Park Portfolio model. Five parks feed into one Park Adoption Team: a low-income rural park, a gentrifying historically Black park, a high-income suburban park, a middle-income high-density park, and a green space crossing several neighborhoods. The team works in both directions with a Park Activators team and connects out to non-profit partners, local government, neighbors and donors." caption="Collaborative Park Adoption teams, with adopters representing each neighborhood, working alongside similarly resourced Park Activator teams." %}

The portfolio model's strengths:

  • Challenges the NIMBYism and resource hoarding of the current model
  • Rebalances how social and political capital are distributed
  • Creates room for organic mentorship, resource sharing and specialization
  • Adds accountability to, and oversight from, other Park Adopters

The model also calls for a Park Activators program, resourced as well as the Park Adopters. The activators we met, event planners, were inspired, devoted and effective, yet felt blocked at every step from putting on free, inclusive events in local parks. Public events were the most reliable way we saw people challenge assumptions about who belongs in parks, and institutional support for them would go a long way.
